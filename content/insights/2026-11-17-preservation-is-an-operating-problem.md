Most conversations about preserving affordable housing focus on financing: tax credits, bonds, subsidy and refinancing. Those matter. But a building that's well financed and poorly run will still lose ground, and the losses show up long before the regulatory agreement expires.

## How affordability erodes

It starts small. Recertifications fall behind, so subsidy payments are delayed. Vacant units wait months for turns. Deferred maintenance becomes a capital problem. Service contracts renew above market. Each issue is manageable on its own; together they drain the reserves a property needs to stay affordable and in good condition.

## What preservation-minded operations look like

**A compliance calendar that runs itself.** Every certification date, inspection and report tracked, with files reviewed before they're due.

**Capital planning tied to operations.** Renovations scheduled around turnover, priced against a standard scope and managed to budget. At Parkchester, a $15 million-plus apartment renovation program finished $3 million under budget because scope and procurement were disciplined from the start.

**Staffing matched to the work.** Regulated properties need people who understand the rules, and enough of them to keep up.

> Financing preserves a building on paper. Operations preserve it in practice.

Owners who treat operations as part of their preservation strategy keep more units affordable, in better condition, for longer.
