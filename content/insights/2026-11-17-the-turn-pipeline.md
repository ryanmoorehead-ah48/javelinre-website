When an affordable property has a large number of vacant units sitting for three or four months, the first instinct is to blame turn capacity: not enough porters, not enough painters, a contractor who doesn't show. Sometimes that's true. More often, the unit is waiting on something other than paint.

A vacant unit moves through four hands before it produces rent. Maintenance makes it ready. Leasing finds an applicant. Compliance certifies the household. Management signs the lease and hands over keys. Each step has its own queue, its own definition of done, and usually its own spreadsheet. The delays live in the gaps between them.

## Map the pipeline before you fix it

Start with a simple board of every vacant unit and the date it entered each stage. Within a week you will see where units pile up. It's common to find units that are physically ready but waiting on an applicant file, or applicants approved but waiting on a unit that maintenance thinks is lower priority.

> Every unit should have one owner at every moment, and everyone should know who that is.

## Three changes that usually move the number

**Run leasing and turns in parallel.** Start marketing and pre-qualifying when the move-out notice arrives, not when the unit is ready.

**Standardize the scope.** A fixed turn scope with a price list removes the back-and-forth that stalls approvals and makes bidding the work straightforward.

**Hold a weekly vacancy meeting.** Fifteen minutes, every unit, every stage, one owner each. The meeting is the system.
